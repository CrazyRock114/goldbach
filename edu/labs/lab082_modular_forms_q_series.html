✨ From Circle Method to Modular Forms

In 1918, Ramanujan and Hardy realized that counting partitions is equivalent to analyzing the boundary singularities of complex functions on the unit disk.

By letting $q = r e^{i \theta}$, the prime series $P(q) = \sum_{p} q^p$ becomes a complex power series. On the boundary circle $r = 1$, the function does not converge; instead, it develops an infinite forest of sharp singular spikes at every rational angle $a/b$!

Because the spikes at denominators 1, 2, 3, 6 are the tallest, integrating around the circle captures these "major arc" resonances, producing the Hardy-Littlewood singular series formula $\mathfrak{S}(N)$.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Slide radius $r$ from 0.70 to 0.98. What happens to the spikes near the boundary?
As $r \to 1$, the geometric series nears its boundary singularity. The spikes become razor-sharp and narrow, visually isolating the Major Arcs from the flat Minor Arc sea!
Challenge 2 Why is the spike at angle $0$ ($q = 1$) the largest of all?
At $\theta = 0$, all terms $q^p = r^p$ are purely real and positive! There is zero destructive interference, creating the absolute maximum amplitude.
Challenge 3 Why does modular symmetry prevent minor arcs from canceling completely in Strong Goldbach?
Because in degree 2 ($P(q)^2$), the $L^2$ norm of the minor arcs is $\sim \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}$, identical in scale to the main term! In Weak Goldbach ($P(q)^3$), the extra power dampens the minor arcs down to $\mathcal{O}(N^2 / \ln^3 N)$, allowing proof.

📐 Boundary Asymptotics & The Farey Dissection

Near a rational boundary cusp $q = r e^{2\pi i a/b}$ with $(a, b) = 1$:

P(r e^{2\pi i a/b}) \sim \frac{\mu(b)}{\phi(b)} \frac{1}{1 - r} \quad \text{as } r \to 1^-

Squaring the series produces coefficients $\frac{\mu(b)^2}{\phi(b)^2} (1 - r)^{-2}$. Integrating $P(q)^2 q^{-N-1} dq$ along a Farey dissection circle of radius $r = 1 - 1/N$ asymptotically recovers the Goldbach singular series $\mathfrak{S}(N)$.

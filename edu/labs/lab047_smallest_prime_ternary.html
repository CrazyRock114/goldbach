Lab 047: Smallest Primes in Ternary Partitions

In binary Goldbach, the smallest prime can be as high as 19 or 73. But in ternary partitions, can EVERY odd number $\ge 9$ be unlocked by the tiny prime 3? Test the "Universal 3" phenomenon!

99
Does p₁ = 3 work? YES!
Ternary Decomposition
99 = 3 + 7 + 89
Even Remainder N - 3: 96 (24 pairs)

Because 96 is even and satisfies binary Goldbach ($96 = 7 + 89 = 13 + 83 = \dots$), prime 3 successfully unlocks 99!

✨ Intuitive Concept: The Universal Prime 3

In Lab 037, we saw that the even number 98 was stubborn: it rejected prime 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, and 17, demanding $p=19$.

Now look at its odd neighbor: 99! Can 99 be unlocked by 3?
$99 - 3 = 96$. 96 is a multiple of 6, blessed with an abundance of pairs: $96 = 7 + 89$! So $99 = 3 + 7 + 89$ works immediately!

In fact, across the entire number line, except for 7 ($7 = 2+2+3$), EVERY single odd number ever tested can use 3 as its first prime! The number 3 is the universal master key of odd partitions.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Why is $N = 7$ the ONLY odd number where $p_1 = 3$ fails to find two more primes?
If $p_1 = 3$, the remaining sum is $7 - 3 = 4$. To write 4 as the sum of two primes, the only option is $2 + 2$. So $7 = 3 + 2 + 2$. But if we require primes in ascending order $p_1 \le p_2 \le p_3$, the smallest prime must be 2 ($2 + 2 + 3 = 7$)!
Challenge 2 Slide to $N = 101$. Find a ternary partition using 3.
$101 - 3 = 98$. And from Lab 037, we know $98 = 19 + 79$! Therefore, $101 = 3 + 19 + 79$!
Challenge 3 Could any odd number $>7$ ever require $p_1 > 3$?
Only if $N - 3$ were an even counter-example to the Strong Goldbach Conjecture! Since no such even counter-example has ever been found up to $4 \times 10^{18}$, $p_1 = 3$ succeeds unconditionally for all known odd numbers!

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

Let $p_{\min}^{(3)}(N) = \min \{p_1 \in \mathbb{P} \mid \exists p_2, p_3 \in \mathbb{P}: p_1 + p_2 + p_3 = N\}$. Under the Goldbach hypothesis $\mathcal{S}$:

p_{\min}^{(3)}(N) = \begin{cases} 2 & \text{if } N = 7 \\ 3 & \text{if } N \ge 9 \text{ and } N \in 2\mathbb{N}+1 \end{cases}

The minimal prime in the ternary problem is effectively a step function with a single discontinuity at $N = 7$.

✨ The Summit of Modern Sieve Theory

In the 1920s through 1960s, sieve theorists chased Goldbach by relaxing the primality condition to "almost primes" $P_r$ (numbers with at most $r$ prime factors):

  • Brun (1920): Proved "9 + 9" (every large even number is the sum of two numbers, each with $\le 9$ factors).
  • Rényi (1947): Proved "$1 + c$" for some constant $c$.
  • Pan Chengdong & Wang Yuan: Pushed bounds to "$1 + 4$" and "$1 + 3$".
  • Chen Jingrun (1966/1973): Reached the ultimate pinnacle: "1 + 2"!

Chen proved that every large even number is either $p + p'$ (a true Goldbach pair!) or $p + (q_1 \cdot q_2)$ (a prime plus a semiprime). To date, "1 + 2" remains the closest humanity has ever reached to the full conjecture.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Set $N = 14$. Identify which decompositions are (1 + 1) and which are (1 + 2).
$14 = 3 + 11$ (1+1, both prime), $14 = 7 + 7$ (1+1, both prime). But also $14 = 5 + 9 = 5 + (3 \times 3)$ is a valid (1+2) Chen decomposition!
Challenge 2 Why does adding semiprimes $(q_1 \cdot q_2)$ bypass the classical "parity barrier" of sieve theory?
Chen introduced a brilliant "switching principle": by sieving weights over both primes and almost primes simultaneously, the parity obstruction cancels out, allowing rigorous positive lower bounds!
Challenge 3 For $N = 30$, how many total Chen decompositions are there?
$N=30$ has 3 true Goldbach pairs ($7+23, 11+19, 13+17$) plus multiple (1+2) semiprime pairs ($5+25 = 5+5^2$, $19+11$, etc.), yielding over 6 Chen solutions!

📐 Formal Statement of Chen's Theorem (1973)

Let $P_2$ denote an integer with at most two prime factors counted with multiplicity ($\Omega(n) \le 2$).

\forall N \in 2\mathbb{N}, N \ge N_0: \quad \sum_{\substack{p + P_2 = N \\ p \in \mathbb{P}, \Omega(P_2) \le 2}} 1 \ge 0.67 \prod_{p > 2, p \mid N} \frac{p-1}{p-2} \prod_{p > 2} \left(1 - \frac{1}{(p-1)^2}\right) \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}

Because the lower bound coefficient $0.67 \mathfrak{S}(N) \frac{N}{\ln^2 N} > 0$ for all large $N$, Chen's theorem guarantees an abundance of $(1+2)$ representations asymptotically.

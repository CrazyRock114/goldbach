Classroom Story: The Lost Notebooks of Mathematical Titans

Before Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the Prime Number Theorem, he was a 15-year-old boy writing lists of primes in a small notebook, counting how many primes lived in blocks of 1,000 integers.

Srinivasa Ramanujan filled three legendary notebooks with over 3,000 formulas without formal proofs, derived purely through numerical intuition, pattern recognition, and divine passion.

Every great mathematician begins as an empirical explorer logging discoveries and searching for hidden structures in numbers!

Hypothesis Discovery Challenge

Challenge 1: Test the hypothesis: "For every even number $2N \ge 8$, there is always a Goldbach pair with $p \le \sqrt{2N}$." Does it hold up to $N=10,000$?

Yes! For numbers up to 10,000, $p_{\min}(2N) \le 47 \le \sqrt{2N}$. In fact, numerical tests up to $4 \times 10^{18}$ show that the smallest Goldbach prime grows extremely slowly, bounded well within $\log^3(2N)$ and easily beneath $\sqrt{2N}$.

Challenge 2: Test: "Is $p=3$ always part of a Goldbach pair for $2N = 6k$?" What is the smallest counterexample?

Counterexample found at $2N = 96$! Because $96 - 3 = 93 = 3 \times 31$, which is composite. For $p=3$ to form a pair, $2N - 3$ must be prime, which fails whenever $2N - 3$ is composite.

Empirical Evidence vs. Mathematical Proof

Why isn't verifying Goldbach's conjecture up to $4 \times 10^{18}$ considered a proof?

$$\text{Mertens Conjecture: Verified to } 10^{10}, \text{ yet FALSE near } 10^{30}!$$ $$\text{Pólya Conjecture: Smallest counterexample is } 906,180,359!$$

Even astronomical empirical success cannot substitute for deductive proof in number theory, where subtle biases can accumulate over transfinite scales.

✨ Intuitive Concept: The Modulo 3 Massacre

Why do numbers divisible by 6 get so many more pairs than their immediate neighbors?

Because all odd primes $>3$ have remainders 1 or 5 modulo 6 (which means remainders 1 or 2 mod 3). When you test candidate prime $p$ against target $N$:

  • If $N \equiv 2 \pmod 6$: Any prime $p \equiv 5 \pmod 6$ produces a complement $N - p \equiv 2 - 5 \equiv -3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$. It is divisible by 3 and instantly disqualified!
  • If $N \equiv 4 \pmod 6$: Any prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 6$ produces $N - p \equiv 4 - 1 \equiv 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$. Disqualified by 3!
  • If $N \equiv 0 \pmod 6$: $(1) + (5) = 6 \equiv 0$. Neither group is divisible by 3! Both sets of primes survive!

Multiples of 6 escape the modulo 3 massacre entirely, receiving twice the prime candidates!

The average of neighbors is $(4+3)/2 = 3.5$, while 60 has 6 pairs (a bonus factor of $6 / 3.5 \approx 1.71\times$!).
Challenge 2 What happens when $k$ itself is divisible by 5 (like $k=5 \implies 30$ or $k=10 \implies 60$)?
It gets a Double Bonus! Because $N$ is divisible by 2, 3, AND 5 ($30k$), it escapes lockout from 2, from 3, AND from 5, boosting its pair count even higher!
Challenge 3 Calculate the theoretical limit of this bonus factor using the Hardy-Littlewood formula.
For the prime $p=3$, the Euler factor in the singular series is $\frac{3-1}{3-2} = \frac{2}{1} = 2$. Multiples of 3 have this factor, while non-multiples of 3 do not. The theoretical bonus is exactly 2.000×!

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

Let $\mathfrak{S}(N)$ be the Hardy-Littlewood singular series for binary Goldbach:

\mathfrak{S}(N) = 2 C_2 \prod_{p \mid N, p > 2} \frac{p-1}{p-2}

For $N \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, the prime $p=3$ divides $N$, so the product includes the factor:

\frac{3-1}{3-2} = 2

For $N \not\equiv 0 \pmod 6$, $3 \nmid N$, so this factor is absent (value 1). Consequently, the asymptotic ratio between multiples of 6 and generic even numbers is strictly 2:1.

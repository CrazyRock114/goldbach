✨ Why Upper Bounds Preceded Lower Bounds

In mathematics, proving that a function never exceeds a ceiling is often vastly easier than proving it never drops to zero!

Using the **Selberg Sieve**, mathematicians proved in the mid-20th century that the number of Goldbach pairs satisfies a strict upper bound:

g(N) \le C \cdot \mathfrak{S}(N) \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}

Notice how the divisor structure of $N$ enters the equation: numbers with many prime factors (like 60, 120, 180) have large values of the singular series $\mathfrak{S}(N)$, which directly lifts both the true pair count and the sieve ceiling!

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Compare $N = 60$ with $N = 64$. Why does 60 have so many more divisors and Goldbach pairs?
$60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5$ has 12 divisors and odd prime factors 3 and 5, boosting $\mathfrak{S}(60) \approx 2.67$. In contrast, $64 = 2^6$ has 7 divisors and NO odd prime factors, with $\mathfrak{S}(64) = 1.0$!
Challenge 2 Does actual $g(N)$ ever pierce through the Selberg upper bound curve?
Never! The Selberg sieve is a mathematically proven upper bound: $g(N) \le C \mathfrak{S}(N) \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}$ holds unconditionally for all even $N \ge 4$.
Challenge 3 What is the fundamental obstacle preventing sieve theory alone from proving $g(N) \ge 1$?
The famous "Parity Problem" identified by Atle Selberg: classical sieve weights cannot distinguish between numbers with an odd number of prime factors vs. an even number of prime factors!

📐 The Selberg Sieve Theorem for Prime Pairs

Let $\Lambda_N = \{p (N - p) \mid p < N\}$. By optimizing the quadratic form $\sum_{d_1, d_2} \lambda_{d_1} \lambda_{d_2} \frac{N}{[d_1, d_2]}$, Selberg's $\Lambda^2$ sieve yields:

g(N) \le (8 + o(1)) C_2 \prod_{p \mid N, p > 2} \frac{p-1}{p-2} \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}

Bombieri and Davenport later sharpened the leading constant from 8 down to approximately 3.99. This guarantees that Goldbach pairs can never grow faster than the asymptotic singular envelope.

✨ The Magic of Generating Functions

In combinatorics and algebra, a generating function is a clothesline on which we hang a sequence of numbers.

By defining $P(x) = \sum_{p \in \mathbb{P}} x^p$, each prime $p$ is encoded in the power of $x$. When polynomials are multiplied, the law of exponents dictates that powers ADD: $x^p \cdot x^q = x^{p+q}$.

Therefore, squaring $P(x)$ automatically computes all possible prime pair sums simultaneously! The coefficient of $x^N$ counts how many ways $p + q = N$. This transforms an additive number theory problem into an algebraic multiplication problem!

Challenge 1 Why are almost all odd-degree coefficients in $P(x)^2$ equal to 0 or very small?
Because the sum of two odd primes is ALWAYS even! The only way $p + q$ can be odd is if one of the primes is 2 (e.g. $2+3=5, 2+5=7, 2+11=13$).
Challenge 2 At cutoff $M = 23$, which even degree $N$ achieves the highest coefficient?
Multiples of 6 (such as 24 and 30) form prominent spikes in the bar chart, showcasing the multiple-of-6 bonus in coefficient form!
Challenge 3 If $P(x)^2$ has coefficient 2 for $x^{10}$, why is $g(10) = 2$?
$10 = 3 + 7 = 7 + 3$ (counted twice in polynomial expansion) and $10 = 5 + 5$ (counted once: $x^5 \cdot x^5$). Unordered pairs: $\{3, 7\}$ and $\{5, 5\}$, exactly 2 pairs!

📐 Formal Cauchy Convolution Formula

Let $a_n = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n)$ be the prime indicator sequence. The generating series $P(x) = \sum_{n=2}^\infty a_n x^n$ has Cauchy product:

P(x)^2 = \sum_{N=4}^\infty \left(\sum_{k=2}^{N-2} a_k a_{N-k}\right) x^N = \sum_{N=4}^\infty r_2(N) x^N

Here $r_2(N) = \sum_{p+q=N} 1$ represents the number of ordered Goldbach representations. Goldbach's conjecture is equivalent to the assertion that $r_2(2k) \ge 1$ for all integers $k \ge 2$.

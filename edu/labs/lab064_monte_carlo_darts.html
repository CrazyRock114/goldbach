✨ Monte Carlo Simulation of Additive Lattice Hits

In the Monte Carlo method, mathematicians use simulated randomness to estimate physical properties and geometrical intersections.

Here, the dartboard is a 2D coordinate grid with side length $N$. Each dart has prime coordinates $(p, q)$ chosen uniformly at random from the set of primes less than $N$.

The Goldbach line is the diagonal $x + y = N$. Any dart landing on this line represents an empirical Goldbach pair! Because there are $\pi(N)$ primes under $N$, the total sample space of pairs is $\pi(N)^2$. The probability of hitting the line is precisely $\frac{2 g(N)}{\pi(N)^2}$.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Throw 500 darts at $N = 50$. Did at least 5 darts hit the Goldbach line?
For $N = 50$, there are $\pi(50) = 15$ primes, so $15 \times 15 = 225$ possible prime coordinate points. Since there are 4 unordered pairs ($3+47, 7+43, 13+37, 19+31$) giving 8 ordered points, the hit probability is $8 / 225 \approx 3.55\%$. In 500 throws, expect $\approx 18$ hits!
Challenge 2 Why are all the darts on the board confined to prime coordinates rather than uniformly scattered everywhere?
Because our random generator only picks primes! This restricts the darts to the prime cross-lattice $\mathbb{P} \times \mathbb{P}$, demonstrating that Goldbach is a question about lattice intersections.
Challenge 3 Increase $N$ to 100 and observe how the empirical hit rate changes.
As $N$ grows, $\pi(N)$ grows, expanding the lattice area $\pi(N)^2$ faster than $g(N)$, so the single-throw probability decreases as $\sim \frac{\ln^2 N}{N}$.

📐 Probability Density on Prime Product Spaces

Let $\Omega_N = \mathbb{P}_{\le N} \times \mathbb{P}_{\le N}$ be the discrete sample space with uniform measure $\mu(p, q) = \frac{1}{\pi(N)^2}$. The hit event $H_N = \{(p, q) \in \Omega_N \mid p + q = N\}$ has measure:

P(H_N) = \frac{r_2(N)}{\pi(N)^2} \sim \frac{2 \mathfrak{S}(N) \frac{N}{\ln^2 N}}{(N / \ln N)^2} = \frac{2 \mathfrak{S}(N)}{N}

Because $P(H_N) \sim \Theta(1/N)$, independent Monte Carlo sampling converges to the exact singular series coefficient $\mathfrak{S}(N)$ by the Law of Large Numbers.

✨ Intuitive Concept: The Solo Four

Imagine numbers as puzzle boxes. For almost every even number, there are multiple different keys (pairs of prime numbers) that can unlock it. For example, 10 can be unlocked by 3 + 7 or 5 + 5!

However, the ancient integers 4, 6, 8, and 12 are special: each has exactly ONE unique Goldbach key. Once you pass 12, prime density and modular options expand rapidly, guaranteeing that every even integer $N > 12$ has at least 2 pairs.

Notice 10 was skipped from the Solo Four! $10 = 3 + 7 = 5 + 5$, making it the first even number to break free and enjoy multiple representations.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Why is 10 NOT in the Solo Four? Find both of its Goldbach pairs using the slider above.
$10 = 3 + 7$ (using twin primes 3 and 7) and $10 = 5 + 5$ (using the midpoint prime 5). Thus, $k(10) = 2$, disqualifying it from the Solo Four!
Challenge 2 What is the first even integer that has at least FOUR distinct Goldbach pairs?
The answer is 34! Its four pairs are: $3+31=34$, $5+29=34$, $11+23=34$, and $17+17=34$. (Even number 24 has 3 pairs: $5+19, 7+17, 11+13$; 34 is the first even number to reach 4 distinct pairs!)
Challenge 3 Why can't $N=14$ have only one pair? Test 14 on the slider.
$14 = 3 + 11 = 7 + 7$. Primes below 14 are $\{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13\}$. Testing complements: $14-3=11$ (prime!), $14-7=7$ (prime!). Thus $k(14)=2$.

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

Let $g(N)$ be the Goldbach counting function denoting the number of unordered pairs of primes $(p, q)$ such that:

g(N) = \#\{(p, q) \in \mathbb{P}^2 \mid p \le q \text{ and } p + q = N\}

Conjecture (Solo Four: Verified up to $4 \times 10^{18}$):

\{N \in 2\mathbb{N}_{\ge 4} \mid g(N) = 1\} = \{4, 6, 8, 12\}

For every even integer $N \ge 14$, extensive computational checks (verified up to $4 \times 10^{18}$) confirm $g(N) \ge 2$, with the asymptotic lower envelope growing as $\Omega(N / \ln^2 N)$.

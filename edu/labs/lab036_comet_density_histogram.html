✨ Intuitive Concept: The Three Bell Curves

In raw counts, $g(N)$ seems to fluctuate chaotically between low valleys and high skyscrapers. But what happens if you divide by the overall growth rate $N / \ln^2 N$?

The chaos instantly condenses into three beautifully neat peaks!

  • Peak 1 (Center $\approx 0.66$): Numbers that are NOT divisible by 3 ($6k \pm 2$).
  • Peak 2 (Center $\approx 1.32$): Numbers that ARE divisible by 3 ($6k$). Notice this is exactly $2 \times 0.66$!
  • Peak 3 (Center $\approx 1.76$): Numbers divisible by both 3 and 5 ($30k$)! ($1.32 \times 4/3 \approx 1.76$).

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Why is there a clear "valley" (gap) between Peak 1 ($\approx 0.66$) and Peak 2 ($\approx 1.32$)?
Because an integer is either divisible by 3 or it is not! There is no "half-divisibility" by 3. This sharp arithmetic dichotomy creates the pronounced valley separating the two peaks.
Challenge 2 Slide sample size up to 2,000. Do the peaks get sharper or blurrier?
The peaks become sharper and more distinct! As $N$ grows, the random fluctuations become small compared to the dominating modular factors.
Challenge 3 Can any integer ever have a normalized ratio $\rho < 0.5$?
For small numbers like $N=4$ or $N=6$, edge effects can occur, but asymptotically, the minimum ratio is strictly bounded below by $C_2 \approx 0.6602 > 0.5$!

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

Define the normalized density random variable $X_N = \frac{g(N)}{N / \ln^2 N}$. The probability measure on $\{4, 6, \dots, N\}$ decomposes as a finite mixture of conditional distributions:

dF(x) = \sum_{r \in (\mathbb{Z}/30\mathbb{Z})} \frac{1}{30} dF_r(x)

Each conditional distribution $F_r$ converges weakly to a Dirac measure concentrated at $\frac{1}{2} \mathfrak{S}(r)$, proving the multi-modal limit distribution.

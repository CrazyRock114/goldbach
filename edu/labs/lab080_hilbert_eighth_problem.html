ICM Paris 1900: Problem #8

"Problems Concerning Prime Numbers"

David Hilbert grouped the distribution of prime numbers into a single grand challenge: proving the Riemann Hypothesis, the Twin Prime Conjecture, and Goldbach's Conjecture.

✨ Why Is Strong Goldbach So Stubborn?

In 1900, Hilbert famously ranked the difficulty of three problems: he predicted that the Riemann Hypothesis would be solved in his lifetime, Fermat's Last Theorem perhaps by the youngest listeners in the audience, but transcendental numbers (like $2^{\sqrt{2}}$) not for centuries.

He was completely backwards! Gelfond solved the transcendence problem in 1934, Andrew Wiles conquered Fermat in 1994, but Goldbach remains standing.

Why? Because multiplication and addition live in two different worlds in number theory. Primes are defined by multiplication (divisibility), while Goldbach asks about their addition ($p + q = 2k$). Bridging multiplicative structure to additive structure is the deepest challenge in modern mathematics.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Which of Hilbert's prime problems was partially solved in 2013?
Two major milestones occurred in 2013! Harald Helfgott proved the Weak Goldbach Conjecture, and Yitang Zhang proved bounded gaps between primes (approaching the Twin Prime Conjecture).
Challenge 2 Why did solving Weak Goldbach not automatically solve Strong Goldbach?
In Weak Goldbach, having THREE prime variables gives an extra integral dimension in the circle method ($S(\alpha)^3$), which provides enough smoothing to overwhelm error terms. In Strong Goldbach ($S(\alpha)^2$), the minor arc errors are of the exact same order of magnitude as the main term!
Challenge 3 What mathematical tools developed since 1900 bring us closest to the finish line?
The Hardy-Littlewood Circle Method (1920), Brun and Selberg Sieve Theory (1920-1947), Chen's Switching Principle (1973), and Deligne's proof of the Weil Conjectures (1974).

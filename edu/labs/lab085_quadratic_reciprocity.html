✨ The Mirror of Quadratic Solvability

Suppose you want to know if $x^2 \equiv p \pmod q$ has a solution. In the 18th century, you had to test every single number up to $q$.

In 1796, at age 19, Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the miraculous Law of Quadratic Reciprocity: whether $p$ is a square modulo $q$ is completely tied to whether $q$ is a square modulo $p$!

The symbols $(p/q)$ and $(q/p)$ are ALWAYS EQUAL, with only one single exception: when both $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, the symbols have OPPOSITE signs! This enables computing square roots modulo giant primes in milliseconds.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Select $7 + 23 = 30$. Why do the two Legendre symbols have opposite signs?
$7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $23 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$! Because both primes leave remainder 3 upon division by 4, $(-1)^{\frac{7-1}{2}\frac{23-1}{2}} = (-1)^{3 \times 11} = -1$. The symbols must flip signs!
Challenge 2 Select $13 + 17 = 30$. What are their residues modulo 4?
$13 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $17 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Since at least one is $1 \pmod 4$, $(13/17) = (17/13)$. Both symbols are identical!
Challenge 3 Look at Eisenstein's lattice box. What do the points inside represent?
Gotthold Eisenstein proved the theorem by counting integer grid points inside a rectangle of size $\frac{p-1}{2} \times \frac{q-1}{2}$. The diagonal line splits the points into two triangles corresponding directly to $(p/q)$ and $(q/p)$!

📐 Formal Statement of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity

For distinct odd primes $p, q \in \mathbb{P}_{>2}$, the Legendre symbol $(a/p) \equiv a^{(p-1)/2} \pmod p$ satisfies:

\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}

With supplementary laws $\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}$ and $\left(\frac{2}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}$. This reciprocity law was the progenitor of Artin Reciprocity and the modern Langlands Program.

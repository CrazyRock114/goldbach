Lab 084: Primitive Roots & Cyclic Groups mod $p$

Every prime modulus $p$ possesses a magical generator $g$: its powers $g^1, g^2, g^3, \dots, g^{p-1} \pmod p$ visit every single non-zero number in a perfect cyclic loop! Explore cyclic group structure in Goldbach primes.

✨ The Clock that Visits Every Room

Imagine a clock with $p - 1$ numbers. Most numbers, when multiplied repeatedly, get trapped in small sub-loops.

For example, modulo 7, the number 2 cycles: $2^1 = 2$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^3 = 8 \equiv 1$. It gets stuck visiting only $\{1, 2, 4\}$ (a subgroup of size 3)!

But the number 3 is a Primitive Root! Its powers cycle: $3^1=3, 3^2=2, 3^3=6, 3^4=4, 3^5=5, 3^6=1$. It visits every single non-zero residue before repeating! Gauss proved that every prime has exactly $\phi(p - 1)$ primitive roots.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 For $p = 7$, test $g = 2$ vs $g = 3$. Why is 3 a primitive root while 2 is not?
$2^3 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so the order of 2 is only 3. But $3^k \pmod 7$ has order 6 ($p-1$). Since its order is maximal, 3 generates the entire group!
Challenge 2 How many primitive roots does $p = 11$ have?
The group order is $p - 1 = 10$. The number of primitive roots is $\phi(10) = 4$. The four primitive roots modulo 11 are $\{2, 6, 7, 8\}$!
Challenge 3 Why does primitive root cyclicity matter for Goldbach's conjecture?
Cyclic structure transforms multiplication into addition via discrete logarithms ($\log_g(a \cdot b) = \log_g a + \log_g b$), allowing Fourier analysis over finite abelian groups!

📐 The Structure Theorem for $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$

By Gauss's theorem in Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801):

(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong C_{p-1} \implies \exists g \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times \text{ such that } \operatorname{ord}_p(g) = p - 1

The number of generators is precisely $\phi(p - 1) > 0$. Any polynomial equation $x^d \equiv 1 \pmod p$ has exactly $\gcd(d, p-1)$ roots, eliminating pseudo-periods in arithmetic progressions.

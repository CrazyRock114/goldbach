Lab 016: Fermat's Sum of Two Squares vs. Goldbach

Pierre de Fermat proved which primes are the sum of two squares ($a^2 + b^2 = p$). How does his solved theorem compare to Goldbach's unsolved sum of two primes ($p + q = N$)?

✨ Intuitive Concept: Solved vs. Unsolved

Both theorems explore decomposing numbers into sums of two special objects:

  • Fermat's Two Squares: Breaks a prime into two squares ($p = a^2 + b^2$). Fermat discovered that this is possible if and only if $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (or $p=2$)! For example, $13 = 2^2 + 3^2$ works, but $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ can never be written as two squares.
  • Goldbach's Two Primes: Breaks an even number into two primes ($N = p + q$). Unlike Fermat's strict mod-4 restriction, Goldbach conjecture states that every single even number works! Yet despite 280+ years of trying, a full proof remains undiscovered.

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Test $n = 29$. Can it be written as two squares? Can it be written as two primes?
• Fermat: $29 = 2^2 + 5^2 = 4 + 25$ (✔ Yes! $29 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$).
• Goldbach: $29$ is ODD! An odd number cannot be the sum of two odd primes; it would need $2 + 27$, but 27 is composite ($3 \times 9$), so $29$ has NO two-prime representation.
Challenge 2 Test $n = 30$. Can it be written as two squares? What about two primes?
• Fermat: Squares $\le 30$ are $\{1, 4, 9, 16, 25\}$. No two sum to 30! (✖ No).
• Goldbach: $30 = 7 + 23 = 11 + 19 = 13 + 17$ (✔ Yes, 3 different ways!).
Challenge 3 Find a number under 50 that satisfies BOTH Fermat ($a^2+b^2$) and Goldbach ($p+q$).
To satisfy Goldbach, it must be even! To satisfy Fermat, it must be the sum of two squares. For example: $10 = 1^2 + 3^2$ and $10 = 3 + 7 = 5 + 5$; or $34 = 3^2 + 5^2$ and $34 = 3 + 31 = 5 + 29 = 11 + 23 = 17 + 17$!

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

Fermat's Two-Square Theorem: For an odd prime $p \in \mathbb{P}_{\text{odd}}$:

p = a^2 + b^2 \iff p \equiv 1 \pmod 4 \iff \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = 1

This is proven using Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: $p = (a+bi)(a-bi)$ splits into two conjugates. In contrast, primes in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ do not form a principal ideal domain, explaining why Goldbach's conjecture has resisted algebraic factorization techniques.

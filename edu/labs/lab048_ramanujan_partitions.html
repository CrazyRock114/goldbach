Lab 048: Ramanujan Partitions $p(n)$ vs. Goldbach

Srinivasa Ramanujan unlocked unrestricted integer partitions $p(n)$, which explode exponentially ($p(100) = 190,569,292$). How does his combinatorial forest compare to the delicate prime stream of Goldbach?

✨ Intuitive Concept: The Forest vs. The Needle

If someone asks: "How many ways can you break 20 into whole numbers?", the answer is 627! If you ask for 40, the answer jumps to 37,338! Ramanujan showed that unrestricted partitions explode like an uncontrollable wildfire ($e^{\pi \sqrt{2N/3}}$).

Goldbach asks something infinitely more strict: You can ONLY use prime numbers, and you can ONLY use exactly two of them!

Out of all 627 ways to partition 20, exactly 2 survive the Goldbach filter ($3+17$ and $7+13$). Finding Goldbach pairs is like finding precious gems scattered across Ramanujan's vast combinatorial jungle!

🎯 Try This! (Hands-On Challenges)

Challenge 1 Compare $N = 30$. What is the ratio between $p(30)$ and $g(30)$?
$p(30) = 5,604$, while $g(30) = 3$. The ratio is $5,604 / 3 = 1,868\text{ to } 1$! Over 99.94% of integer partitions are non-Goldbach.
Challenge 2 At $N = 40$, how many total partitions exist?
$p(40) = 37,338$! Meanwhile, Goldbach has only $3$ pairs ($3+37, 11+29, 17+23$).
Challenge 3 Who discovered the asymptotic formula for $p(n)$?
Srinivasa Ramanujan and G. H. Hardy in their historic 1918 paper, using the very first version of the Circle Method!

📐 Mathematical Rigor & Formal Definition

The generating function for unrestricted partitions $p(n)$ is Euler's product:

\sum_{n=0}^\infty p(n) q^n = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1 - q^k} \implies p(n) \sim \frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}} e^{\pi \sqrt{2n/3}}

In contrast, Goldbach's generating function involves prime indicators $\sum_{p} q^p$, which lacks modular invariance under the full modular group $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, explaining why binary Goldbach remains far more difficult than Ramanujan partitions.
